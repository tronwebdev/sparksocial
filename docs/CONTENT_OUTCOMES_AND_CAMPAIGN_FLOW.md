# SparkSocial AI — Content Outcomes & Campaign Setup Flow

**Version:** 1.0
**Audience:** Product + engineering team (already building)
**Purpose:** Define exactly *what content SPARK produces for whom*, and *how a campaign is set up and generated*. This is the content definition, not the architecture.

---

## PART 0 — The one idea everything hangs on

Before the detail, the frame that makes the rest make sense.

An influencer is the easy case because **their proof is their face.** Clone the face, clone the voice, generate content. Done.

Every other business also has a proof asset — it's just not a face. That is the only reason their content looks different.

| Business | What proves they're good | So their content is made of |
|---|---|---|
| Influencer / coach | Their face, voice, opinions | Themselves, performing |
| SaaS | The product working | Screen recordings + outcomes |
| Agency | Client results | Before/afters + case numbers |
| Freelancer | Their portfolio + judgment | Work artifacts + narration |
| Local business | Their craft and space | Real footage of the work |
| E-commerce | The product in real use | Product footage + customer proof |

Once you see it that way, a second thing falls out — and this is the part that determines whether SparkSocial actually works or just produces noise:

### There are three ways SPARK can produce a post. Not one.

**1. SYNTHESIZE — SPARK makes it from nothing.**
Avatar video, voice clone, AI-generated image or video, text-only post, quote card.
Use when the proof asset is a person, or when generated visuals are genuinely fine.

**2. ASSEMBLE — SPARK builds it from things they already own.**
Product screenshots, portfolio files, testimonials, past photos, docs, existing footage.
Nothing new gets filmed. SPARK edits, sequences, captions and packages.
Use when the proof already exists in digital form.

**3. DIRECT + FINISH — SPARK tells a human exactly what to film, then finishes it.**
SPARK sends a precise 20-second shot brief. The owner films on their phone. SPARK trims, stabilizes, captions, adds the hook, adds music, exports in three aspect ratios, schedules it.
Use when the proof is physical and cannot be generated.

**This third mode is the whole reason a local business stays subscribed.** Every AI social tool on the market does mode 1 only. That's why a barbershop signs up, gets an AI avatar saying "book your appointment today," and cancels in three weeks. What actually works for that shop is 15 seconds of clippers finishing a fade — and no model can generate it.

If we run the capture loop, we're the only ones. If we don't, we're another avatar tool.

## PART 1 — What content is actually valuable, by business type

This is the core of the document. For each segment: what they own, what content genuinely moves their business, concrete examples of individual posts, and the production mode each requires.

Read the "Example posts" columns literally — these are the outputs SPARK should be capable of producing.

---

### 1.1 B2B SaaS

**What they own:** the product interface, customer outcomes, the founder's point of view, usage data.
**What they're starved of:** product-in-motion. Almost every SaaS company under-produces the single highest-converting thing available to them — short clips of the product actually doing something.

| Content type | What it looks like | Mode | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Workflow clip** | 15–30s screen recording of ONE workflow. Hook: "This used to take my team four hours." Then the workflow. Then the result. | Assemble | Highest intent-to-trial conversion of anything they can post. Under-produced everywhere. |
| **Problem-first education** | 30–60s on the *pain*, product never mentioned until the last 3 seconds — or not at all. "Your reps aren't slow. Your handoff is." | Synthesize / Assemble | Reaches people who don't know the category exists. Builds the top of funnel. |
| **Customer outcome card** | Real number, real logo, one sentence of context. "Cut response time from 6 hours to 4 minutes." | Assemble | Proof. Converts the already-interested. |
| **Founder POV / thesis** | Founder (or avatar) stating a strong opinion about the industry. Not about the product. | Synthesize | Distribution engine. People follow founders, not SaaS logos. |
| **Ship / changelog post** | "New this week: X." Screen recording of the new thing. | Assemble | Signals momentum. Reactivates dormant trials. Cheap to make, weekly cadence. |
| **Comparison (X vs Y)** | Honest side-by-side against a known alternative — including where the alternative wins. | Assemble | Captures the highest-intent audience there is: people actively shopping. |
| **Myth-bust** | "Everyone says you need X. You don't. Here's what actually matters." | Synthesize | High save rate, high comment rate. |
| **Use-case series** | Same product, told for one specific role or industry. "If you run a 3-person agency, here's the setup." | Assemble | Lets one product speak to five audiences without five products. |
| **Data insight** | Aggregate anonymized platform data. "We looked at 40,000 emails. Follow-ups sent Tuesday get 31% more replies." | Assemble | Earns press and shares. Nobody else has your data. |

**Mix:** ~50% educational/problem, 25% product, 15% proof, 10% founder POV.

**The critical AI requirement for this segment:** SPARK must actually *understand the product.* It has to ingest the help center, docs, landing pages, and real screen recordings — and it must refuse to state specifics it cannot ground in those sources. A SaaS founder who catches SPARK describing a feature that doesn't exist will churn immediately and tell people. Ground every product claim. When ungrounded, generalize or ask.

### 1.2 Agency

**What they own:** before/afters, client results, a way of thinking, a team.
**Structural note:** an agency has **two content jobs** — content for themselves, and content for each client. Same engine, separate brains, hard separation of assets. This is what the Agency Portal is actually for.

#### Content for the agency itself

| Content type | What it looks like | Mode | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Before/after transformation** | Split or wipe transition. Old → new. No narration needed. | Assemble | Outperforms everything else available to an agency. Instantly legible. |
| **Client result snippet** | "$4k ad spend → 61 booked calls in 30 days." Screenshot of the dashboard, blurred where needed. | Assemble | The only content that closes deals directly. |
| **Case study breakdown** | 60–90s: the problem, what we tried, what worked, the number. | Assemble | Builds authority. Converts on longer time horizon. |
| **Teardown of another brand** | Analyze someone else's landing page, ad, or brand — publicly, constructively. | Synthesize + Assemble | Massive reach. Free positioning. The single most underused agency format. |
| **Process / how we think** | "Here's how we choose which channel to test first." | Synthesize | Builds authority **and filters bad-fit leads** before the sales call. |
| **"We said no to this"** | A project they turned down and why. | Synthesize | Signals standards. Attracts better clients. Very high engagement. |
| **Team / culture / hiring** | Faces, workspace, how the team works. | Direct + Finish | Agencies sell teams, not tools. Buyers want to see who they'd work with. |
| **Offer / availability** | "Two onboarding slots in September." | Assemble | Creates scarcity. Agencies chronically forget to post this. |

**Mix:** ~40% proof/case, 30% POV/teardown, 20% process, 10% offer.

#### Content the agency produces *for clients*
Runs the full engine per client genome. Each client is whatever they are — a restaurant client gets local-business content, a SaaS client gets SaaS content. The agency operator's experience is oversight: approve, adjust, report. Assets never cross between clients.

### 1.3 Local business (barbershop, restaurant, salon, tailor, gym, clinic, auto shop, café)

**What they own:** a physical space, a craft, staff, regulars.
**What they need:** almost entirely **Direct + Finish.** An avatar will not save a barbershop.

| Content type | What it looks like | Mode | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Craft capture** | 15–25s of the work itself. The fade finishing. The dough stretching. The seam being sewn. The engine coming apart. No talking. | Direct + Finish | The entire game. Hypnotic, endlessly repeatable, algorithm-friendly, proves skill without a single claim. |
| **Transformation** | Before → after. Haircut, nails, a repaired item, a fitted garment. | Direct + Finish | Locally the highest-converting format that exists. |
| **Staff personality** | 20s of a team member being themselves. Name on screen. | Direct + Finish | People buy from people locally. Turns a shop into "Emeka's place." |
| **Space / atmosphere** | The room at opening. Morning light. Setup. Sound on. | Direct + Finish | Reduces the anxiety of walking into somewhere new. |
| **Customer reaction** | The moment someone sees the result. Consent required. | Direct + Finish | Social proof that cannot be faked or bought. |
| **Offer / urgency** | "Tuesday 2pm–5pm, 20% off cuts this week only." | Assemble | Direct bookings. Must be scheduled deliberately, not spammed. |
| **Behind the prep** | Opening routine, ingredient delivery, tools laid out, restock. | Direct + Finish | Cheap to film, builds familiarity, humanizes the business. |
| **Local / seasonal context** | Neighborhood events, weather, holidays, local slang. | Synthesize + Assemble | Signals "we're actually here," which is the whole point of a local account. |
| **FAQ / price transparency** | "How much is a full set?" answered plainly on camera. | Direct + Finish | Removes the #1 barrier to a DM. Massive save rate. |

**Mix:** ~50% craft & process, 20% people, 20% offer, 10% community.

**Non-negotiable:** if we can't reliably get 3–5 short phone clips per week out of this owner, the account will fail. The capture loop is not a nice-to-have here — it *is* the product for this segment.

### 1.4 Freelancer (web designer, developer, copywriter, photographer, consultant)

**What they own:** a portfolio, a process, judgment, and a personality.
**Position:** quasi-influencer. Avatar and voice apply fully — but tethered to **work**, not lifestyle. This is the segment most people get wrong by producing generic motivational content.

Using **web designer** as the worked example, since it's the case that keeps coming up:

| Content type | What it looks like | Mode | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Portfolio in motion** | Screen-recorded scroll-through of a site they built, 20s, narrated or captioned, one line about the goal it served. | Assemble | Their portfolio is dead in a PDF. In motion it sells. |
| **Before/after redesign** | Old site → new site, hard cut or wipe. | Assemble | Instantly legible value. Needs zero explanation. |
| **Process breakdown** | "How I choose a font pairing in 90 seconds." Screen recording of real work. | Assemble | Demonstrates judgment, which is what people actually hire. |
| **Hot take / strong opinion** | "Your hero section is three screens too long." | Synthesize (avatar/voice) | Referrals follow personality. This is where the avatar earns its keep. |
| **Teardown** | Redesign a well-known brand's page, live, unsolicited. | Assemble | Highest-reach format available to a freelancer. |
| **Client win** | "Landing page rebuild took conversion from 1.2% to 3.8%." | Assemble | Turns craft into business outcomes — which is what buyers pay for. |
| **Teaching the thing they sell** | "Three CSS tricks I use on every build." Counterintuitive, but it works. | Assemble | Giving away the *how* proves competence. Buyers still don't want to do it themselves. |
| **Behind the build** | Figma timelapse, wireframe → final. | Assemble | Shows the work behind the price. |
| **Availability post** | "Two slots open for September." | Assemble | Converts far better than anyone expects. Freelancers systematically forget to post it. |
| **Pricing / FAQ** | "Why I charge ₦2m for a landing page." | Synthesize | Pre-qualifies leads. Removes the awkward first call. |

**Mix:** ~35% educational, 30% POV/personality, 25% proof/portfolio, 10% offer.

### 1.5 E-commerce / physical product

**What they own:** the product, customer results, a founder story.

| Content type | What it looks like | Mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Product in use** | Real hands, real context, 10–20s, no narration | Direct + Finish / Assemble |
| **AI-UGC style review** | Talking-head review styled as user-generated. **Must be disclosed.** | Synthesize |
| **Unboxing / first impression** | Package → product → first use | Direct + Finish |
| **Problem → solution demo** | The frustration, then the product solving it | Assemble |
| **Founder story** | Why the product exists | Synthesize |
| **Drop / restock** | Urgency, countdown, sold-out signalling | Assemble |
| **Styling / use variations** | Same product, five ways | Assemble |
| **Review reaction** | Real review on screen, response over it | Assemble |

**Mix:** ~35% product, 30% proof/UGC, 20% educational, 15% brand/founder.

**What they own:** a framework, client transformations, opinions.

| Content type | Mode |
|---|---|

### 1.7 Creator / influencer

Fully Synthesize-eligible. Face clone, voice clone, generated b-roll, opinion content, story content, trend participation.

**Important:** this is the *easy* case and it must not be allowed to drive the architecture. If we design for influencers first, we will ship an avatar tool and fail every other segment.

## PART 2 — Two rules that protect quality

### Rule 1: Avatar content must not be the default

If every SparkSocial account leans on avatar video, three things happen: accounts converge on an identical look, platforms suppress the pattern, and our customers' audiences learn to scroll past it.

**Avatar defaults OFF unless the business's proof asset is a person.** Influencers, coaches, consultants, freelancers: on. SaaS: on for founder POV only. Agencies: on for POV only. Local business and e-commerce: off by default.

Cap avatar output as a share of total posts even where it's enabled.

### Rule 2: The ratio matters more than the generation

The most common way an AI social tool fails is not bad writing. It's **posting 100% promotional content.** Look at the mixes in Part 1 — no segment should be above 35% promotional, and local businesses are at 20%.

Getting the mix right is worth more than getting the copy right. A mediocre craft video posted three times a week beats a beautifully written promo posted three times a week, every time.

## PART 3 — The campaign setup flow

Two levels of input: **workspace setup** (once) and **campaign setup** (every campaign). Keep them separate. Don't re-ask workspace questions at campaign time.

---

### 3.1 Workspace setup — five questions, asked once

The failure mode here is a long form. Every additional field costs completion. **Ask five things, infer the rest, show the inferences back as editable chips.**

**Q1 — "Paste your website or Instagram."**
One field. SPARK crawls it and infers: what the business does, category, tone of voice, offer, price tier, location, language, existing visual identity.
*This single input should produce 70% of the profile.*

**Q2 — "Is there someone willing to be on camera?"**
→ Yes, and we can clone their face and voice / Yes, but we'll film them ourselves / No, keep me out of it
*Determines whether Synthesize is available at all.*

**Q3 — "What can you show me?"** (multi-select, icon tiles)
→ My screen / product · My space / premises · My finished work · My physical product · Nothing yet
*This is the most important question in the entire flow.* It determines the production mode, which determines everything downstream.

**Q4 — "What does a good month look like?"**
→ More leads · More bookings · More trials/signups · More sales · Bigger audience · More applicants
*The objective. Drives the mix and the CTA strategy.*

**Q5 — "Show me what you already have."**
Connect existing social accounts (pulls past posts *and their performance*), upload assets, connect Drive, point at a help center or docs.
*Feeds straight into the Asset Library.*

**Then: SPARK plays it back.**

> "Here's what I've got. You're a web design freelancer in Lagos, premium pricing, you work mostly with SaaS and agency clients, your tone is direct and a bit blunt, you're going for leads, and you've got 14 portfolio pieces I can use. You're comfortable on camera. Anything wrong?"

Editable chips. Not a form. This confirmation step is what makes the profile feel like the agent *understood* rather than *collected*.

---

### 3.2 Campaign setup — outcome first, never format first

**The single most important design decision in the product: the unit is not a post. The unit is a campaign tied to a business outcome.**

Do not let the user request "a post." Do not open with a format menu. Open with the outcome.

#### Step 1 — Outcome

> **"What should this campaign actually do?"**

Presets, tuned to the genome:
- Fill quiet days (local)
- Book more discovery calls (agency/freelancer)
- Drive trial signups (SaaS)
- Launch something new (all)
- Sell a specific product (e-comm)
- Grow audience (all)
- Fill a cohort / open enrollment (coach)
- Hire (agency, local)

Plus a free-text option: *"Or just tell me."*

Then two follow-ups only:
- **Target and window.** "How many, by when?" — accept vague ("more than last month") and normalize it.
- **What's the offer or hook?** Only if the outcome implies one.

**Stop there.** No more questions. Everything else SPARK infers or proposes.

#### Step 2 — SPARK proposes the plan (does not ask)

SPARK reads the genome + Asset Library and comes back with a plan, not a form:

> "For 40 bookings in August I'd run 6 posts a week on Instagram and TikTok for four weeks.
>
> **Half of it is craft** — the actual cutting. That's what books appointments for shops like yours.
> **A fifth is your team** — people book people.
> **A fifth is offers** — the Tuesday slow-day promo, twice a week.
> **The rest is neighborhood stuff** — keeps you visible between promos.
>
> I can build 9 of those 24 posts from what you've already given me. For the other 15 I need about 6 minutes of filming from you, total, across the month. Want me to set that up?"

Three things this does: it states the reasoning, it exposes the gap honestly, and it quantifies the human effort required. That last part is what converts.

#### Step 3 — The gap and the capture plan

This is the step nobody else has. Surface exactly what's missing and exactly what it costs to fix:

| What I can make now | What I need | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| 9 posts | — | none |
| +15 posts | 5 short clips | ~6 min total, one sitting |

Then batch the capture requests. **Never send capture briefs one at a time.** A local business owner will not respond to a daily nag. Send one "filming session" per week — 3 to 5 briefs, five minutes, done in one go.

**Capture briefs must be specific enough to film without thinking.**

Bad: *"Post a video of your work today."*
Good: *"Film 20 seconds of the fade from behind the chair. Vertical. Don't talk — we'll add the captions. Face the window so the light's on the client's head. Keep the clippers in frame the whole time."*

Every brief specifies: subject, camera position, orientation, duration, movement, audio, lighting, and what not to do. A brief that fails that bar should not be sent.

Delivery: WhatsApp first for local and Nigerian SMB (matches the Iyobo surface), push/in-app for everyone else. They film on their phone, reply with the video, SPARK finishes it and shows them the result.

**The finish step is what earns the subscription.** Raw phone footage in, publishable post out: auto-trim the dead space at both ends, stabilize, burn in captions, overlay the hook in their brand type, add a licensed audio bed, export 9:16 / 1:1 / 16:9.

#### Step 4 — Review the calendar, not the posts

Show a month view with each slot labelled by *type*, not by copy: `Craft` · `Team` · `Offer` · `Craft` · `Community`.

Let them adjust at the mix level — "less offer, more craft" — and regenerate. Individual post editing should be available but never required. If the user has to open all 24 posts, we've failed.

#### Step 5 — Approval mode

Autopublish ON by default, per the PRD. Offer three settings:
- **Autopublish** — just post it
- **Review first week, then autopublish** — the trust-building default we should actively recommend
- **Review everything** — for agencies and regulated verticals

That middle option is the one that gets people to autopublish. Recommend it explicitly.

#### Step 6 — Run and report against the outcome

Report against the **stated outcome**, not vanity metrics.

> "You wanted 40 bookings. You're at 27 with nine days left. Craft videos are doing 3× your offer posts, so I'm shifting four offer slots to craft for the rest of the month. Also — the clip of Emeka doing the taper got 4× everything else. I want three more like that."

Two things to build in here:

**Learn from their data, not our table.** The mixes in Part 1 are cold-start defaults only. After 60–90 days, the mix should be derived from *that account's* actual performance. Keep a permanent exploration floor of ~20% of slots so the engine doesn't collapse onto whatever won in week three and go stale.

**This is the compounding advantage.** A competitor can copy the content formats in a month. They cannot copy 90 days of a specific customer's performance history.

### 3.3 The end-to-end flow, condensed

```
WORKSPACE SETUP (once)
  URL → infer everything
  3 questions: who's on camera / what can you show / what's a good month
  Connect accounts + assets
  SPARK plays back the profile → user corrects chips
        ↓
CAMPAIGN SETUP (each time)
  "What should this campaign do?" → outcome + target + window
        ↓
  SPARK proposes: volume, platforms, mix, reasoning
        ↓
  Gap report: "9 now, 24 if you film for 6 minutes"
        ↓
  Batched capture session (WhatsApp) → owner films → SPARK finishes
        ↓
  Calendar review at MIX level, not post level
        ↓
  Approval mode → run
        ↓
  Report vs. the stated outcome → reweight the mix → repeat
```

## PART 4 — What "done" looks like

A Lagos barbershop, a Toronto B2B SaaS company, and a freelance web designer in Manila each connect an account, answer five questions, and receive a month of content that a good human marketer would look at and say *"yes, that's right for that business."*

Different formats. Different ratios. Different production modes. And nobody at Tronweb wrote a rule for barbershops, sales software, or freelance web design.

The tell that we've got it right: the barbershop's month is 50% footage of haircuts that SPARK told the owner how to film, and the SaaS company's month is 50% education that mentions the product twice. If both of those come out of the same engine without hand-tuning, the engine works.
